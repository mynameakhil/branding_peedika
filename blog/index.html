Local SEO

Your Google Business Profile matters more than your logo

If your business has a physical location and serves customers in an area, the free listing on Google Maps is the single most valuable digital asset you own. It is free, it appears above paid results, and it is what most people mean when they say "I found it on Google".

Why the listing beats the logo

A logo builds recognition over years. A complete, correct Google Business Profile can start bringing calls this week. When a person searches "tea shop near me" or "bakery in Kozhikode", the map pack appears before ordinary results, and most people never scroll past it. Your business does not need to be the first name in the world — it needs to be in that short list for the searches that matter.

Fill it in completely, in this order

  1. Name. Use the name customers actually use, spelled the way you spell it on the board. Do not add keywords — it looks like spam and it gets your listing suspended.
  2. Category. Pick the most specific category that fits, then add the others that genuinely apply. Category is one of the strongest ranking signals for local searches.
  3. Address and service area. If you deliver or you travel to customers, set up a service area as well as a location.
  4. Hours, including festival variations. Wrong hours produce one-star reviews, and one-star reviews are the most expensive thing on this list.
  5. Phone number. Use a number someone actually answers, and make sure it is the same number that appears on your site and your board.
  6. Photographs. Add ten to fifteen: the outside of the shop, the counter, the product, and the people behind it. Businesses with real photographs get noticeably more calls.
  7. Description. Two or three honest sentences about what you sell, who it is for, and what makes the trip worth it.
  8. Services and products. List what you sell with plain-language names, and include the ones people ask for by name.

Ask for reviews, then actually read them

Reviews are the second biggest lever after photos. The practical way to get them is to ask at the moment of a good experience — a regular customer who is happy, right after a good purchase. Put a small sign at the counter with a QR code to your listing, and say the words out loud. Most people will never volunteer a review; almost none will refuse a direct ask.

Then reply to every one of them, including the critical ones. A calm, specific reply to a bad review does two things: it reassures the next reader, and it shows the reviewer that someone is actually in charge.

Keep it current

Update the listing when something changes — new hours, a new product, a renamed shop. Post photos a few times a month. It takes twenty minutes and it is the cheapest marketing work available. If you want help deciding what to prioritise, ask us and we will tell you honestly whether this is where your next hour should go.

Content

What to photograph on your phone so posts look professional

Professional-looking content is not about having better equipment. It is about repeating the same six shots, in the same good light, with the same framing — every single time.

Light beats everything else

Nearly every bad product photo in the world is a lighting problem. Find a window, put the product beside it rather than in front of it, and turn off the ceiling light. Soft light from one side, no harsh shadows, no second light source fighting the first. If you only take one tip from this article, take that one.

The six shots worth repeating

  • The wide establishing shot. The whole product or counter, showing the context a customer would see in the shop.
  • The straight-on shot. Square to the subject, filling most of the frame, at the height of the product rather than the height of your eyes.
  • The detail shot. Close in on the texture, the label, the packaging, the thing that makes it worth buying.
  • The in-use shot. A hand holding it, someone eating it, a machine working. This is usually the one that sells.
  • The group shot. The people behind the business. Customers buy from people they recognise.
  • The comparison shot. The product next to something familiar that gives a sense of size.

Simple settings that help

Clean the lens — it is the single most common cause of a soft photo. Turn off the flash. Tap the screen on the product so the camera focuses where you want. Avoid the digital zoom; move closer instead. If your phone has a portrait or "food" mode, use it for a soft background, but switch it off for anything where the shape of the product needs to read accurately.

Make it a routine, not a project

Photograph one thing properly every day rather than forty things badly once a month. Save them into a single album named after the business. When you need content, the library is already there and you are choosing from good material instead of scrapping around with whatever the camera roll happens to contain.

A repeatable set of shots, taken consistently, will do more for a small business's feed than any amount of filter. If you want templates that work with these photos, we can build a set for you.

Social media

How to write a caption that gets replies instead of likes

A like is a reflex. A reply is a decision. If you want a post to start a conversation rather than fade into a feed, the difference is usually one question at the end of the caption.

The mistake most businesses make

They write a caption that describes the image. The image already says it. So the caption adds nothing and the reader has no reason to type anything. A caption that describes what the reader should do with the post is far more likely to get an answer.

Five structures that work

  • The genuine question. "What would you order if you could only have one?" — easy to answer, and the answers give you content ideas.
  • The choice. "The 8am dosa or the 8am parotta?" Forgiving, fun, and everyone has an opinion.
  • The local reference. "Anyone else remember when this lane had only two shops?" Local nostalgia pulls local people into the conversation.
  • The useful tip. "If you are new to [the area], here is the fastest way to reach us." Useful posts get saved and shared — the two things that matter more than likes.
  • The honest admission. "We got this wrong last month and changed it." Trust is built by the things businesses admit, not the ones they hide.

Match the language to the reader

Write the caption in the language your customers actually use, in the order they use it. If that is Malayalam, write in Malayalam. If it is the Malayalam-English mix people type in comments, write in that. A perfectly grammatical English caption from a shop whose customers read Malayalam is a caption for nobody.

Reply, even when it is one line

Every comment you answer makes the next person more likely to comment — the thread looks alive, and people join conversations that are already happening. A short reply, a name, an emoji used sparingly and only where it fits your brand: that is enough. What is not enough is silence. A post with twelve comments and no replies from the business looks abandoned, and the next customer decides not to bother asking.

One more thing worth doing: write down the questions people ask you in the shop. Those are your best content ideas, your best website FAQ, and your best advert angles — all of them, for free, from people who are already interested in buying.

Websites

Five things a small-business website must have

A business website does not need to be large. It needs five things, done properly, and a person who can load it on a phone with one bar of signal.

1. An answer in the first screen

What do you do, where do you do it, and how do I contact you? A visitor should be able to answer all three without scrolling. Most small-business sites bury the answer under a slow-loading animation and a paragraph of history.

2. A visible way to reach you

A phone number that is a tap-to-call link, a WhatsApp button, and a short form. Put them where a visitor expects them — in the header area, at the end of every section, and in the footer. If it is buried, most people will not find it, and they will simply try the next result.

3. It loads fast on a phone

More than half of the traffic to a local business site arrives on a phone, often on mobile data. That means images sized for the screen they appear on, and a page that does not wait for a large script to download before showing anything. A fast site is not a technical luxury — it is most of the search ranking advantage you can get for free.

4. Real photographs of the actual business

A stock image of a smiling team in a modern office tells a Kerala customer nothing. A photograph of your shop, your counter and the product itself tells them everything: that you are real, that you are local, and that you are open. It also does more for trust than any paragraph of copy you can write.

5. A way to find you on a map

An embedded map, the full address, and clear directions. A surprising share of local customers check where you are on the map before they travel, even for a business two streets away.

What is not on the list

A visitor does not need an animation, a slider, a chatbot, a 40-page menu of services or a customer testimonials carousel with stock faces. None of those make anyone call you. What makes them call you is clarity, speed and trust — so spend the effort there.

Branding

How to price a branding project without getting taken for a ride

Branding quotes vary wildly, and it is genuinely difficult to tell from the outside whether a high number is fair or padded. A few things help.

Understand what you are buying

A logo on its own is one deliverable. A brand identity is a system: the mark, the supporting marks, the colour palette with defined roles, the type pairing, the rules for using them, and the templates that make it usable. Two quotes can differ by a factor of five and both be reasonable, because one includes the system and the other includes only the first step.

So compare quotes on scope, not on total. Ask each person for a list of what you receive, in a format you can lay side by side. If one quote cannot answer that question clearly, that tells you something useful.

Ask the four questions

  1. What exactly do I receive, and in which formats? You should end up with editable source files, not just a logo in a PDF. Ask what happens if you want to change the colour in three years.
  2. How many rounds of revisions are included? A reasonable engagement includes two or three. A quote that offers unlimited revisions either has a very high price or very little care in it — both are warnings.
  3. Who actually does the work? A one-person studio doing the design, or a studio that briefs a junior designer and a mid-level designer? It is fine either way, but you should know.
  4. What happens to the files? The answer should be: they are yours, fully, with no restrictions. If the designer keeps the working files or requires a licence fee to keep using the logo, walk away.

Be suspicious of certain signals

A price that is dramatically lower than every other quote is not automatically a bargain — it is often a template, a logo mill, or work that stops after the mark itself. Equally, a quote that avoids a written scope is not a bargain at all; it is a quote for something undefined. Ask for a scope in writing, and read it.

Budget for the whole first layer

If a business has no brand, no content and no website, doing all three at once is rarely the right first step — but doing only the cheapest piece can be worse, because you end up with a nice logo on a site that still does not work. The usual pattern is to fund the foundation properly: the identity, a small number of real content templates, and a simple site that converts. Then build on it as the business can afford to.

Whatever you decide, we are happy to look at quotes with you and explain what is included and what is not. There is no charge for that conversation, and if we are not the right fit we will tell you so.
